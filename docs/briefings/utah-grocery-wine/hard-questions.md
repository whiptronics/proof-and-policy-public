# Hard questions

- **Status:** a proposal for review by qualified legal and fiscal experts. Not enacted law, not legal advice, not attorney-reviewed, and not reviewed by a person. Written with AI assistance (Claude) for Ethan Whipple.
- **Generated** from `web/src/lib/framework.ts` by `npm run docs:framework` (in `web/`); edit the data, not this file. Methods: statute-matrix@1.0, policy-designs@1.0.

1. **Would Utah lose alcohol revenue?** It depends on DABS's price to grocers and on what DABS saves, which nobody has measured. With wine at today's shelf price and DABS selling to grocers at 90% of it, the state gives up $4.54 million a year if DABS saves nothing and $0.09 million if its wages fall with sales, with license fees covering the administration; a rise of 7.1% in household wine purchases would cover the first. If grocers set their own prices instead (design B), the state is $0.36 million a year ahead if 30% of household purchases move.
2. **How much would wine cost in grocery stores?** The same as at a state store. Under the framework grocers sell at the price the commission fixes, as state stores and package agencies must (point 6). If a bill let grocers set their own prices instead, the price would depend on the margin they keep; the five designs page models that option.
3. **Would grocery stores actually be willing to sell it?** Not known. A grocer's margin is the gap between DABS's price to it and the shelf price. Pennsylvania's licensees buy at 10% below retail and 559 grocery stores hold its wine permits. Utah's own package agency grid would pay a typical grocery store's wine volume about 25% of its sales, and large stores less.
4. **Could the state collect the same revenue without distributing the wine?** Yes in arithmetic: a retail-level charge equal to DABS's margin collects it (designs C and D). But a private wholesaler's margin then sits on top, so the grocery price rises 51.2%. A retail-level charge with distribution that cost nothing would break even at a 20.6% price premium.
5. **Could direct producer-to-grocery distribution work?** Only if delivery is cheap: D beats B's price only if delivery costs less than 19.8% of the price to grocers, which nothing measures. It also needs new state authority and an answer on federal wholesaler permits.
6. **Would wine consumption increase?** Probably somewhat: more outlets usually mean more buying. Tennessee's wine purchases rose clearly faster than its neighbors' after 2016, though total alcohol's rise was within the usual range. The revenue model counts no added drinking in its main case.
7. **Would this make underage purchases easier?** More outlets mean more places for minors to try to buy. Utah's beer rules (ID before every sale, training, compliance checks, penalties) would extend to wine; how well they work at grocers is what the compliance-check failure rate would measure.
8. **What happens to DABS employees?** No layoffs are modeled. Under B, DABS still buys, stores and delivers every bottle. Store staffing would change only if store sales fall, and no staffing plan exists.
9. **What happens to rural package agencies?** Not estimated. DABS pays each one a fixed amount a year from a published grid, by bottles sold and average bottle price. A licensed grocer in an agency's town could take some of its wine sales, and with them its place on the grid; the framework leaves the agency terms to the bill.
10. **Could local wineries benefit?** Possibly, through grocery shelves under any design, and directly under D. Neither effect is measured.
11. **What would happen to existing state stores?** They would keep spirits and the full wine range. Their wine sales fall by up to 24.6% of DABS's total if 30% of household purchases move.
12. **Would expanded access hurt independent retailers?** Utah has no independent liquor stores: wine is sold only at state stores and contract package agencies. Small grocers could be priced out by flat fees: $1,217 a year is 28.3% of the wine gross margin of a store selling a tenth of the average.
13. **How would licensing and enforcement be funded?** By license fees set to cover the administration, $1,217 a year per store if all 411 grocery establishments were licensed (the cost itself is a placeholder).
14. **What can Tennessee teach Utah?** That grocery wine did not significantly change the number of liquor stores there and raised wine tax receipts, by a peer-reviewed estimate. Tennessee taxes wine by excise; Utah earns a monopoly margin, so the revenue result does not carry over.
15. **Why might Colorado's experience differ?** Colorado converted every beer retailer's license automatically, convenience stores included, and has private wholesalers and an excise tax. Its first years have no government evaluation yet.
16. **Could the change affect funding for education or other programs?** Yes, through the dedicated deposits figured on DABS's liquor sales. Under B it depends on whether sales to grocers count; under C and D the bill must allocate part of the charge to keep them whole.
17. **Why not allow grocery stores to sell all distilled spirits too?** Spirits are outside this proposal and nothing here analyzes them. The Community Guide recommends against further privatization of retail alcohol sales where government controls them, on strong evidence that it raises per capita consumption; grocery wine is a partial step of that kind, and spirits would be a larger one.
18. **What evidence would undermine the proposal?** Grocers needing margins that push prices far above state stores; very few households switching (so fees, not revenue, carry the program); compliance-check failures above beer's; or a measured rise in alcohol harms where grocery wine began.
19. **What assumptions are still unverified?** Every number is software-transcribed and unreviewed. The share that would switch, DABS's avoidable costs, the administration cost, grocers' margins and delivery costs are assumptions or placeholders.
20. **What laws must change?** Design B needs 8 amendments and leaves 2 legal questions open; C needs 10, D 10 and E 12. The change matrix lists each with its current text.
