Grocery wine in Utah: design options and fiscal effects

Legislative and technical briefing · Proof & Policy · draft, model policy-designs@1.0

Draft for review by qualified legal and fiscal experts. Computed by the project's models from FY2025 DABS figures that software transcribed and no person has reviewed. Not a fiscal note, not legal advice, not attorney-reviewed; written with AI assistance (Claude). The maintainer supports grocery wine; the analysis reports favorable and unfavorable results alike.

Executive summary

  • Five designs were compared: today (A), DABS selling to licensed grocers (B), licensed private wholesale with a state charge (C), producers supplying grocers (D), and a hybrid (E). None of B to E meets every constraint: the state breaking even in every switching case with no store savings, a grocery price within 10% of a state store, and a 28% grocer margin.
  • B is the best-supported design. With no store costs counted as saved, the state is +$0.36M a year ahead if 30% of household wine purchases move; −$0.20M if price-sensitive shoppers mostly stay; −$0.50M if almost none move, which is the new administration (a placeholder). Fees that cover the administration remove that loss.
  • Wine can sell at today's price. With DABS selling to grocers at 90% of its shelf price and grocers charging the state-store price, as state stores and package agencies must (32B-2-503(2), -605(7)), the state gives up $4.54M a year if DABS saves nothing and $0.09M if its wages fall with sales (fees cover the administration). Half of Utahns live within 0.8 miles of a grocery store and 2.0 of a state store.
  • B needs 8 statutory changes and leaves 2 legal questions open; C and D need 10, E 12.

Current law

  • Wine is liquor under Title 32B; an off-premise beer retailer license (32B-7) covers beer only. Wine is sold to take home only at state stores and contract package agencies.
  • DABS must mark wine up at least 88.5% over landed case cost (32B-2-304(2)(b)). Utah has no wine excise; the state's revenue is DABS's margin and sales tax.
  • Manufacturers may sell liquor in Utah only to the department or military installations (32B-11-208); out-of-state sellers ship only to the department (32B-4-401(2)).
  • Federal law (27 U.S.C. 203, 205) requires basic permits for importers, producers and wholesale buyers and restricts suppliers' inducements to retailers; the text read does not require three tiers.

Proposed legal design (framework: DABS wholesale at today's shelf price)

  • A commission-issued grocery wine license with local consent, on the pattern of the off-premise beer state license (32B-7-401 to -409); grocery stores only in a first phase (411 grocery establishments in 2023).
  • DABS sells sealed wine to licensees below its shelf price, at a price the bill sets apart from the markup floor; grocers charge the state-store price.
  • Beer's safeguards extended to wine: training, ID on every sale (electronic at the register), display rules, compliance checks, penalties; a seller's minimum age set in the bill.
  • Fees covering the administration ($1,217 a year per store if all 411 were licensed); yearly DABS reporting; a review after three years.

Impacts on government revenue

  • Net change counts DABS's margin and any charge, sales tax, store costs saved (zero in the headline) and the new administration. Gross receipts are not net revenue.
  • Dedicated deposits are figured on DABS's liquor sales (32B-2-304(5), -305(4)); under B, whether sales to grocers count is open; under C and D a charge must be allocated to keep the funds whole.
  • Break-even under B with no savings: at least 17.5% of household wine purchases must move to grocers to cover the administration; at a 10% premium, grocers could keep a margin of only 8.5%.
  • State stores lose up to 24.6% of DABS's wine sales if 30% of household purchases move; rural package agencies' effect is not estimated.

Public-health and enforcement considerations

  • The Community Preventive Services Task Force recommends limiting outlet density (2007) and recommends against further privatization of retail sales where government controls them (2011). More outlets are not harmless; prices above state stores damp the effect by an unmeasured amount.
  • Already required for off-premise beer: a state license, proof of age before every sale, staff training, compliance-check reimbursement, penalties for sales to minors. New for wine: limits on eligible stores, electronic ID at the register, promotion rules, program reporting.
  • Indicators: compliance-check failure rates, licenses by county, wine and total alcohol volume, crashes and injuries, DABS revenue, dedicated deposits, store and agency sales. A change after adoption does not show the law caused it.

Relevant interstate evidence

  • Tennessee: food stores licensed to sell wine (July 1, 2016). The abstract reports no statistically significant change in the number of liquor stores, higher wine excise-tax revenue, and mixed evidence of a short-term fall in liquor-store employment whose significance depends on the method. Peer-reviewed estimate (model-based).
  • Colorado: beer retailer licenses converted to beer-and-wine licenses (March 1, 2023). Proposition 125 (statutory initiative) automatically converted every fermented malt beverage retailer license (grocery and convenience stores) to allow wine and tastings. The Department of Revenue's liquor pages refused automated access, and no government or peer-reviewed evaluation of the 2023 change was found. Not read.
  • Pennsylvania: wine expanded permits (Act 39 of 2016) (Approved June 8, 2016). A control state that is still the wholesaler and runs about 560 state stores. Permits on June 30, 2025: 980 convenience stores, 559 grocery stores, 296 restaurants, 62 hotels, 9 mass merchants. Law and official figures only.

Tennessee and Colorado tax wine by excise and have private wholesale; a Tennessee percentage does not transfer to Utah's monopoly margin. Pennsylvania, where the state stays the wholesaler, is the closest structure to B.

Legal changes required (design B)

  • M01 Definitions: wine is liquor. Utah Code 32B-1-102(68), (143).
  • M02 A grocery wine license. Utah Code 32B-7-401 to -409 (the off-premise beer state license).
  • M03 DABS as supplier to grocers. Utah Code Title 32B, Chapter 2 (32B-2-501, -601, -603).
  • M10 Local authority. Utah Code 32B-7-201.
  • M11 Operating rules. Utah Code 32B-7-202.
  • M12 Training. Utah Code 32B-1-701, -703, -704.
  • M13 Electronic ID checks. Utah Code 32B-1-407.
  • M15 Compliance checks and violation records. Utah Code 32B-7-305; 32B-1-705.

Open for B: M09 deposits to dedicated funds; M17 local sales tax.
